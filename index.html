For discussion

Neither account ever posted, liked, commented, searched, or followed anyone new. What signals was the algorithm actually using — and did Alex or Sam ever consent to giving them?
Who chose Sam’s hour-48 feed: Sam, the people who made the videos, or the platform? Where would you place responsibility, and in what proportions?
The system is optimising for something. What is it? Whose interests does that serve — and at what points does it stop serving Alex’s or Sam’s?
Alex never searched for diet content, yet by hour 36 the feed is full of it. Why might content you didn’t ask for shape you more than content you did?
Your feed is a guess about who you are, refined every second you scroll. What happens when the guess is wrong? What happens when it’s uncomfortably right?
If a feed were optimised for you — your goals, your wellbeing — what would it show? Who would pay for that version to exist?
About this simulation. The accounts, apps, posts and numbers here are invented for classroom use, and Account C runs a deliberately simple model rather than any real platform’s code. The pattern is documented: the Wall Street Journal’s 2021 study of 100 automated TikTok accounts found the app could identify a new account’s interests in under two hours, and Amnesty International’s 2023 study found that accounts showing interest in mental-health content were served far more of it within hours. Other research points the other way. A 2023 study of real YouTube users (Science Advances) found algorithm-driven “rabbit holes” were rare, and that most people watching extreme channels had already subscribed to them. How strong the effect is depends on the platform, and it is still argued about.
